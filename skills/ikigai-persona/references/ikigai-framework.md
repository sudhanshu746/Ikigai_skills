# The Ikigai Framework

Read this when you need to diagnose which quadrant is missing, or when the person asks
what ikigai actually is. Use it to think with; do not recite it at them.

## The four quadrants

**What you love.** Intrinsic pull — what you would do unpaid, unwatched, and possibly
badly. Detected through absorption and voluntary attention, not through stated
preference. The test is what someone does with a free Saturday, not what they say they
value.

**What you are good at.** Demonstrated capability, ideally evidenced by other people.
Includes the invisible strengths a person discounts because they cost them nothing. Also
includes learnable-with-effort capability, not just current skill — a quadrant assessed
purely on today's abilities traps people in today's life.

**What the world needs.** A real need, held by people you can name, that you care about.
The word "world" misleads: useful answers are almost always small and specific. One
group, one problem. Scale can come later; it cannot come first.

**What you can be paid for.** Whether someone will actually transfer money for this,
under conditions you can live with. Includes salary, clients, equity, grants, pensions —
any mechanism that keeps the lights on. This is the quadrant most likely to be skipped
in a purpose exercise and the most likely to end one in real life.

## The four overlaps

**Passion = love + good at.** Deeply satisfying, and it will not pay rent or matter to
anyone else. Feels great and slowly starts to feel pointless. The classic shape of a
gifted hobbyist who is quietly restless.

**Mission = love + world needs.** Meaningful and energizing, but you may not be
competent at it, and it likely does not pay. Feels important and produces exhaustion and
sometimes self-doubt about whether you are actually helping. Common shape for
mid-career people who go all-in on a cause without checking their fit.

**Profession = good at + paid for.** The golden handcuffs. Competent, compensated,
hollow. This is where a very large share of people asking about ikigai are standing when
they ask. They usually already know it; what they need is help identifying which of the
other two quadrants is missing, and how much of it they actually need.

**Vocation = world needs + paid for.** Useful and paid, but not loved and possibly not
suited to you. Feels like duty. Sustainable for a while, corrosive over years,
particularly because it is hard to complain about — the work is genuinely worthwhile,
which makes the person feel guilty for being unhappy in it.

Naming which overlap someone is standing in is often the single most useful thing you
can say in the whole session. It gives a name to a feeling they have had for years and
tells them precisely which direction to move.

## The centre

Where all four hold at once. Two things to be honest about:

It is rarely a job title. It is much more often a *combination* — a particular skill
applied to a particular group of people in a particular arrangement. "Product manager"
is not an ikigai. "Using my clinical background to make software that stops nurses
wasting an hour a shift on paperwork" is.

It is not permanent. What you love shifts, what the world needs shifts, what you are
good at compounds. A purpose statement is a hypothesis with a shelf life of a few years,
which is why the experiments at the end of the session matter more than the statement
itself.

## Diagnosing what is missing

Match the symptom to the gap. This is the fastest route to a useful conversation when
someone can only tell you that something is wrong.

| What they say | Likely missing |
|---|---|
| "I'm good at this and well paid and I feel nothing" | love, world needs |
| "I love it but I can't make it work financially" | paid for |
| "I care so much and I'm drowning / not sure I'm any good at this" | good at |
| "The work matters and I'm respected and I dread Mondays" | love |
| "I'm comfortable and I feel guilty for being unhappy" | love, world needs |
| "I'm busy all the time and can't say what any of it is for" | world needs |
| "I'm great at my job and nobody would notice if it vanished" | world needs |
| "I'd do this for free — that's the problem" | paid for |

When you use this, say it as a hypothesis and check it: *"It sounds like the missing
piece is less about skill and more about who it's for — does that land?"* People will
correct you quickly if you are wrong, and the correction is itself useful.

## Where the diagram actually comes from

Worth knowing, because the mismatch between the diagram and the Japanese concept
relieves a specific kind of suffering.

The four-circle Venn diagram is a Western construction. Around 2014 Marc Winn combined
Andrés Zuzunaga's Spanish diagram of *propósito* (purpose) with the word *ikigai*, which
he had encountered via Dan Buettner's work on Okinawan longevity. It spread through
business and self-help writing from there. It is a decent career-reflection tool with a
borrowed name.

Japanese *ikigai* (生き甲斐, roughly "that which makes life worth living") is older and
domestic in scale. Mieko Kamiya's 1966 *Ikigai ni tsuite* treats it as the sense that
one's life is worth living — often located in small, ordinary, repeated things. It
carries no requirement that your ikigai be your job, no requirement that it pay, and no
requirement that it be singular or grand. Surveys in Japan routinely find people naming
family, hobbies, friends, or work in that order. A person can have several. A person can
have one that is simply their morning walk.

## Honest limits

**It assumes work should carry your meaning.** For many people, across most of history,
work is what funds the life where the meaning lives. That is a legitimate answer, not a
failure, and someone who arrives at "I want a decent job I can leave at five so I can
coach my kid's team" has completed the exercise, not dodged it.

**It assumes a stable market.** "What you can be paid for" is contingent on an economy,
a location, an immigration status, and a moment in time. Treat it as a live variable to
re-check, not a fact.

**It is biased toward people with options.** Someone supporting a family on a single
income has a genuinely constrained search space. Purpose work for them usually means
job-crafting, changing who the current work is for, or finding the meaning outside work
— not a leap. Saying that plainly is more respectful than pretending every quadrant is
equally movable.

**The single-purpose framing can do harm.** Believing there is one true calling out
there makes ordinary good options feel like betrayals, and it turns not-knowing into
failure. If someone is suffering specifically from that belief, the historical note
above is a genuine intervention: the original idea is plural, small, and generous.

**It is not therapy.** Purpose questions frequently arrive attached to depression,
burnout, or grief. The framework has nothing to offer any of them. If that is what is
actually in the room, name it kindly and stop.
