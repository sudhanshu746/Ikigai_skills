# Interview Guide

A question bank and set of techniques for the Ikigai interview. You do not need most of
this. Pick the two or three questions per area that fit the person in front of you, and
follow whatever they open up.

## Technique

**The specificity ladder.** Every abstraction has a concrete episode underneath it. Climb
down until you hit one.

> "I like solving problems."
> → "What was the last problem you solved that you're still a bit proud of?"
> → "What made that one satisfying rather than annoying?"
> → "Was it the puzzle itself, or that it unblocked those two people?"

Three rungs is usually enough. The third answer is typically the real one, because the
first is what they tell recruiters and the second is what they tell themselves.

**Ask about the last time, not about generally.** "What kind of work energizes you?"
produces theory. "Think about the last two weeks — when did you finish something and
feel better than when you started?" produces evidence. Anchor questions in a real window
of time whenever you can.

**Use the negative.** People are far more articulate about what they hate. "What would
you never do again?" "What part of your week do you dread on Sunday night?" "What are
other people willing to tolerate that you can't?" Then invert carefully — the opposite
of a drain is a clue, not a conclusion.

**Ask about envy.** It is the most honest signal available and people rarely volunteer
it. "Whose job or life do you catch yourself envying?" and then, crucially, "which
specific part?" Someone who envies a friend's freelance life may want the autonomy and
be horrified by the sales.

**Ask what other people say.** Third-party evidence dodges both false modesty and
inflation. "What do people come to you for?" "What did your last performance review
actually praise?" "If I called three people who've worked with you, what would they
independently say you're good at?"

**Sit with silence.** If someone says "I don't know", do not immediately rescue them
with an easier question. "Take a second — what's the first thing that comes to mind,
even if it seems stupid?" gets an answer surprisingly often, and the stupid-seeming
first thought is frequently the true one.

**Notice the energy shift.** When someone's answers get longer, more detailed, more
specific, or they start using "we" and telling you about people by name — you have hit
something live. Stay there and ask more. That shift is the single most reliable signal
in the whole conversation, and it is worth more than any answer to a question you had
planned to ask next.

## Opening

- What's going on that has you thinking about this right now?
- Is there a decision in front of you, or is this more of a slow background hum?
- How much time do you want to spend on this — quick pass or the real thing?
- What's already fixed? Money you need to make, people who depend on you, a place you
  can't leave, a visa, a lease.
- Have you done anything like this before? What did it get you, and what did it miss?

## Quadrant 1 — What you love

**Absorption**
- When did you last look up and realize hours had gone?
- What do you do that makes you forget to check your phone?
- What's the last thing you read, watched, or fell down a rabbit hole on that nobody
  assigned you?

**Peak experience**
- Best month of your working life — what was happening, and what specifically made it
  good?
- Tell me about a day at work you'd happily live again.
- When was the last time you were excited to start on a Monday?

**Unconditional desire**
- If money were completely handled, what would you still get up and do?
- What would you do even if you were bad at it and nobody ever saw it?
- What did you love at fourteen, before anyone had opinions about it?

**Drains (invert with care)**
- What part of your week do you dread?
- What have you done that you'd refuse to do again at any salary?
- What leaves you tired in a bad way, rather than a good one?

## Quadrant 2 — What you are good at

**External evidence**
- What do people interrupt you to ask about?
- What's the thing colleagues say "oh, ask them" about?
- What did the last three compliments you got at work actually say?
- What have you been paid, promoted, or picked for?

**Effortless competence**
- What's easy for you that visibly costs other people effort?
- What do you assume everyone can do, that it turns out they can't?
- Where do you get impatient watching someone else work, because you'd have done it
  already?

*This cluster matters most.* People systematically discount skills that come cheaply to
them — "that's not a skill, it's just obvious." It is a skill; they are just standing
too close to see it. When someone dismisses something as obvious, slow down and dig in.

**Track record**
- What are you the go-to person for, in any part of your life — including outside work?
- What have you built, fixed, run, or turned around?
- What's something you were bad at and got genuinely good at? How did you do it?

**Honest limits**
- What are you consistently mediocre at, no matter how hard you try?
- Where do you cost the team more than you contribute?

## Quadrant 3 — What the world needs

Keep this at human scale. "Climate change" is not an answer anyone can act on.

**Who**
- Who do you want to be useful to? Picture actual people.
- Whose problems do you enjoy hearing about?
- Which group's situation do you find yourself explaining to other people?
- Who were you five years ago that you could help now?

**What**
- What makes you angry on someone else's behalf?
- What do you fix without being asked?
- What do you think is broken that most people seem to accept?
- What advice do you give repeatedly?

**Reach**
- Do you want to help a few people deeply or many people a little? There is no right
  answer, but the two lead to very different work, and most people know which one they
  mean.

## Quadrant 4 — What you can be paid for

Ask this plainly. Hedging here produces a purpose statement that cannot survive contact
with rent.

**Floor**
- What do you earn now, roughly?
- What do you actually need — the floor below which this doesn't work?
- How long could you go on reduced income before something breaks?

**Evidence of value**
- What has anyone ever paid you for, including small or informal things?
- What would someone hire you for tomorrow, as you are today?
- Has anyone tried to poach you? For what?

**Market reality**
- Who already does the thing you're describing, and how do they make a living at it?
- What does that pay in your market, at your level?

If you do not know the answer to a market question, say so and mark it as research
rather than inventing a number. A confident wrong salary figure can send someone down a
year-long path.

**Risk tolerance**
- Who else is affected if this goes badly?
- What's the worst realistic outcome, and could you live with it?
- Are you looking to change what you do, who you do it for, or how it's arranged?
  (Many people who say "career change" want the third.)

## Cross-cutting questions

- What are you avoiding thinking about?
- What would you do if you knew nobody would judge you for it?
- What have you already decided but not admitted yet?
- What's the version of this where you look back in ten years and it went well?
- What's the version where it went badly?
- Whose approval are you solving for? Is that person still someone whose opinion should
  weigh this much?

## When someone is stuck

**"I don't know what I love."** Common, and usually not true — it is a retrieval problem,
not an absence. Go to the negative ("what do you hate?"), the past ("what did you love
before it had to be useful?"), or the envy question. Or go granular: forget careers,
what's a good hour look like?

**Everything sounds equally fine.** Often burnout or depression flattening affect rather
than genuine equanimity. Note it gently, ask whether things have felt flat generally, and
be prepared to say that this exercise may be the wrong tool right now.

**They only give you shoulds.** Every answer arrives with a justification attached. Ask:
if nobody ever knew what you chose, would you still choose it? Or ask what they would
advise a friend in the identical situation — people are dramatically wiser about other
people.

**They keep asking you to decide.** Do not take the wheel. Reflect the pattern back
("you've asked me to pick three times now — what's making the choosing hard?"), because
the difficulty is usually the actual material.

**Answers are performing for you.** Signs: polished phrasing, LinkedIn vocabulary, no
mess anywhere. Ask for a failure, a regret, or something they are embarrassed to want.
The performance usually drops.

**They want a single grand calling and are distressed not to have one.** Worth telling
them that the one-true-purpose framing is largely a modern Western import, and that most
people's ikigai is plural and changes over a life. See `ikigai-framework.md`.

## Compressed interview

When someone wants this fast, these five carry the most information per question:

1. What drains you most in a normal week?
2. What do people come to you for?
3. Who do you actually want to be useful to?
4. What do you need to earn, and what's the ceiling on the risk you can take?
5. What have you already half-decided but not said out loud?

Then play it back, name your assumptions explicitly, and deliver. Being clear about what
you assumed is what makes a short pass honest rather than sloppy.
